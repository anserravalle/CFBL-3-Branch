Finding the Right Words

Letters From the Practice  ·  July 2026

 

Dear friends,

July has a way of loosening things. The school calendar falls away, the evenings stretch out, and the pace that carried us through spring finally eases. For many of the families I work with, summer is the first real chance in months to exhale. It is also, quietly, a season of noticing. When we slow down, we begin to feel what we have been carrying.

I have been thinking a great deal lately about the words we use for what we carry. In my evaluation work, I sit with people who have spent years trying to name their experience, reaching for language that almost fits but never quite does. They arrive with a word they found online or borrowed from a friend, hoping it will finally explain them. Sometimes it does. Often it points them in the wrong direction.

There is real relief in being understood precisely. Not roughly, not approximately, but accurately. The right word opens a door. It tells you where to go, what kind of help to look for, and what you can reasonably expect. The wrong word, even a gentle one, can quietly close that same door and send a person down a path that was never going to fit.

This is not about policing language or correcting anyone. It is about the difference between something that is hard and something that is genuinely interfering with a life, and about giving each its due. Both deserve compassion. They simply do not call for the same response.

Summer gives us a little more room to notice that difference. My hope this month is that you find some of that room, and that when you reach for words to describe your own experience, you let yourself be precise. Clarity is its own kind of care.

Warmly,
Dr. Niki Serravalle

Featured Blog: The Difference Between Clinical and Just Being Hard

Our newest piece on Make It Make Sense takes on a question that comes up constantly in the evaluation room. What separates a clinical condition from ordinary human difficulty, and why do our most popular words fail to tell them apart?

Terms like neurodivergent and high functioning were built for community and dignity, not for diagnosis. They were never clinical, and when we treat them as though they were, people get lost. The essay follows three people who looked similar on the surface and needed completely different things: an introvert in a poorly fitting job, a teenager whose social anxiety was treatable in months, and a woman whose autism had gone unnamed for nearly forty years.

The throughline is simple and important. A clinical deficit is stable, early, and limiting across settings. Difficulty is real and exhausting but workable. Naming the difference is what gets people to the help that actually fits.

Featured Resource: Naming What You Feel

In keeping with this month's theme, the resource we want to share is one of the simplest and most evidence-informed practices we know: affect labeling, sometimes called name it to tame it. The idea is that putting a feeling into specific words actually lowers its intensity, and research on the brain bears this out. When we move from a vague sense of being overwhelmed to a precise name, the nervous system tends to settle.

You can try it in about thirty seconds. The next time you notice distress, pause and ask what this actually is. Not just bad or stressed, but more exactly: disappointed, anxious, lonely, grieving, depleted. Say it or write it in one short sentence. The goal is not to fix the feeling but to know it accurately. Precision, here too, is a form of care.

Community Spotlight: The Quiet Power of Showing Up Together

One of the things we love most about our groups is what happens in the room that no one plans. Someone says out loud the thing they assumed only they felt, and the person across the circle nods. Our Pet Loss Support Group is a good example. That grief is so often carried alone, brushed off as something that should not hurt this much. Sitting with others who understand can be its own kind of medicine. Whether the gathering is about loss, creativity, or simple connection, the value is the same. People stop being alone with something. That is community, and it is at the heart of why we do this work.

Closing Reflection

If there is one invitation in this letter, it is to let yourself be precise. With your feelings, with your struggles, with the words you use to describe your own life. The world will offer you a hundred approximate labels. You are allowed to wait for the one that actually fits. And if you are still searching for it, that is part of what we are here for.

May your July hold a little more ease, a little more room to notice, and the quiet relief of being understood.
