### Page 1 — What a psychological evaluation can actually answer
Most people don't wake up wanting a psychological evaluation. They start with a question that won't go away: *Why has this always been so hard? Why doesn't the usual advice work for me or my child?* A good evaluation answers questions like:
- Is this ADHD, autism, anxiety, trauma, a learning difference — or some combination?
- What are this person's genuine strengths, not just their struggles?
- What specific supports, accommodations, or treatments are likely to help?
An evaluation is not a label for its own sake. It's a map.

### Page 2 — Signs an evaluation may be helpful
- Lifelong difficulty with focus, organization, time, or follow-through
- Feeling "different" socially in ways that are hard to name
- A child who is bright but struggling at school in a way that doesn't add up
- Burnout, overwhelm, or shutdown that keeps returning
- Prior diagnoses or treatments that never quite fit
You do not need all of these. One persistent question is enough reason to ask.

### Page 3 — How to prepare
- Jot down the specific moments that made you seek answers
- Gather past records if you have them (school reports, prior testing, report cards)
- Note family history — these patterns often run in families
- Write down your goals: *What do I want to be different after this?*

### Page 4 — What to bring
- A list of current medications and providers
- Any prior evaluations or IEP/504 documents
- For a child: a teacher's observations if available
- Your questions — every one of them

### Page 5 — Questions people ask
- *How long does it take?* It varies by what we're assessing; we'll tell you up front.
- *Will it be used against us?* No. The results are yours, and we explain every part.
- *What happens after?* You get clear findings and a concrete plan of next steps.

> **You don't have to have it all figured out to start.** If something here sounds familiar, a short consultation can tell you whether an evaluation is the right next step.
